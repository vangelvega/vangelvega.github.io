Prioritizing elevator repairs by the step-free access each outage takes away

In the New York subway, one broken elevator can close a station to anyone who can't take the stairs. Choosing which to fix first means knowing how many riders lose access, not how often an elevator fails.

An elevator with no backup on a critical route can close a whole station to people with reduced mobility. After six years designing transport infrastructure, I asked how a subway control center decides which elevator to fix first. New York's MTA publishes the data I needed to find out.

I designed FlowOps, a decision tool that orders elevator outages by the step-free access each one takes away. The operator approves or changes the order.

At a glance

Problem

Ranking outages by how often an elevator fails points to the wrong station. Lexington Av/63 St has 252 failures, but every elevator there has a backup. One failure never cuts its step-free route.

Solution

A queue ordered by how many daily riders depend on the lost access. Every priority explains itself, entrapments come first, and the operator approves each priority before it becomes a work order.

What the model changes

  • A different order. Lexington Av/63 St drops from 1st to the bottom. 161 St–Yankee Stadium rises from 97th to 1st.
  • One direction counts half. Counting a one-direction loss as half cut total access lost by 29%.

Failure counts point to the wrong station.

Availability rose to 98% in 2025, yet outages hit 17.7 a day, the most in 11 years and 26% above the 2015–2019 average. And 9 of 10 accessible stations can lose their step-free route, fully or in one direction, when a single elevator fails.

I checked three sources:

  • MTA open data (4 datasets, 386 elevators, 7,052 outages in 12 months). Failures point to the wrong station: the one that fails most never loses access.
  • Public audits (NYC Comptroller, MTA Inspector General). An entrapment alert isn't proof, so confirming has to be deliberate.
  • Tool reviews and job posts (Limble, UpKeep, Maximo, HxGN EAM, Fiix). Tools record work orders. None explains why one comes first.

The control center had no shared rule for the order. No public source says how the MTA prioritizes today, so I treat this as an assumption.

Priority measures who loses access, then explains itself.

Maintenance teams rank work by consequence × probability. Here the failure already happened, so I measure consequence: how many daily riders depend on the lost access. A loss in both directions counts in full. One direction counts half. Probability only matters for stations one failure away from losing access.

The hard call: the kind of loss, or how many people it reaches?

Franklin Av (C) loses all step-free access with one failure, and about 1,950 people enter it a day. Times Sq–42 St (1 2 3) loses one direction, which carries about 23,000 entries a day.

  • A
    The kind of loss decides. Franklin goes first, but the number of High stations changes every day.
  • B
    Impact decides. Times Sq goes first, and a small station with no access can sink to the bottom.
  • C
    Impact, with a floor. Times Sq is High and Franklin is at least Medium.

I chose C because it follows the metric and still protects people who can't enter a station at all. Two rules come out of it:

  • A station with no access in both directions is at least Medium.
  • A station that still has access never goes ahead of one that lost it.

Recurrence and age show as reasons, never as points. Outages over 5 days go to the accessibility lead.

Step-free entries are an estimate

The ranking uses total entries. The interface shows estimated step-free entries: 8.3% of riders, the share of New Yorkers with difficulty walking (ACS 2024). It can fall short or overshoot, so it's labeled an estimate and never changes the order.

Ranking by access lost changed what the tool is for.

Maintenance software already records, assigns and closes work orders, so FlowOps is a decision layer: it doesn't assign technicians, and approving a priority creates the work order in the team's own system. It serves the head of operations, who decides and reviews history; the maintenance supervisor, who receives the work order; and the accessibility lead, who owns long outages.

Fast for most outages, deliberate for one.

At 6:00 the head of operations finds 17 stations with an elevator issue, two with people trapped. Most outages need speed: the order ready, decide, move on. Entrapments need caution: an alert isn't proof, and one click would make the most serious decision routine.

How might we give the operator an order they can trust and act on in one click, while keeping the riskiest decision deliberate?

The product has three views

One question each, from right now to the last twelve months.

Network status: what needs a decision right now?

  1. Entrapments is the only count that turns red.
  2. Priority reads as a word: Urgent, High, Medium, Low.
  3. Access says which directions lost the step-free route.

Each count states what it includes, and its number matches the rows in the queue.

Every priority explains itself

  1. What was lost: both directions at 74 St–Broadway.
  2. Who depends on it: 1,746 estimated step-free entries a day.
  3. The rule: High starts at 1,660.

People trapped come first

  1. Red is reserved for Urgent.
  2. A live countdown shows the time left to confirm.
  3. Confirm is its own button, never in bulk.

In July 2010 the control desk got over 7,000 entrapment alerts for 60 real ones, and stopped answering them.

Decide, with a way back

  1. In progress approves the priority and creates the work order.
  2. Dismissed marks a false alarm and asks for a reason.
  3. Undo stays on screen for 10 seconds.

A failed send never loses a decision

  1. A banner says since when the connection is down.
  2. The row keeps its priority and offers Retry.

Map and queue speak the same language

  1. The selected row.
  2. Its station on the map, labeled with what it lost.
  3. Color is priority in both.

History and decision log: is today worse than usual, and who decided what?

The shift is compared with the same weekday over the last month, so a bad morning reads against a baseline. The log rebuilds any decision: what was suggested, what was approved, and when.

Network analysis: where does the network lose the most step-free access?

This is where the model came from. Over a year, 1.9 million estimated step-free entries were lost, 34% in just 5 stations. 8 of the 20 stations that lose the most aren't among the 50 that fail the most.

Why the year's top station isn't in the shift queue

161 St–Yankee Stadium's loss comes from April to August 2026 (September to March has no data), mostly July and August, at 0% during replacement. Outages over 5 days leave the shift queue for the accessibility lead. The shift and the year measure different things.

A visual language borrowed from the subway

Black top rules, line badges and color only where a decision is needed come from NYC subway signage. It's a portfolio choice: in production, FlowOps would follow the control center's own interface.

Where I used AI

Mine: the access-lost metric, options A, B and C and the choice of C, the priority thresholds, the entrapment flow, and what to cut. AI: joining the data, research, simulated walkthroughs and coding.

Data
Joined 4 MTA datasets; tested options A, B and C on real stations.
Research
Audits, reviews and job posts, each source next to its finding.
Walkthroughs
Three AI-simulated profiles (operator, keyboard-only, screen reader) tested each round. Findings are hypotheses, not user evidence.
Prototyping
Coded versions from v0 to v8, built with Claude from my flows and rules, polished in Figma.

Simulated walkthroughs found three problems

Each round went through simulated walkthroughs and automated accessibility checks.

The data made me drop some of my own ideas.

  • I dropped "critical elevators fail more." Without private elevators (42 of 43 log no failures), those with no backup fail 20.1 times a year against 22.7.
  • "One failure closes the station" became "fully or in one direction." Platform elevators work side by side.
  • One rate for the whole city. Borough rates pushed both Yankee Stadium stations up, but where people live doesn't say where they enter.

The model is a hypothesis until an operator uses it on a real shift.

Outage times and the shift are simulated on real MTA data. First I'd test whether operators agree with the order. Would they handle 34 St–Hudson Yards, accessible but busy, before a small station that already lost access?

I'd track:

  • Time from opening the screen to the first approved priority
  • How many priorities change, and why
  • Hours a station stays without step-free access
